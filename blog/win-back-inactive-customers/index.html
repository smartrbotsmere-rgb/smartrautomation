How to Win Back Inactive Customers

By Mere Hicks · October 15, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026

You already know these people. They were regulars, they liked your place, and they showed up without needing a push. Then something changed. Maybe it was a new schedule, a tight month or one off day, and now you haven't seen them in a while.

Winning them back doesn't take a big discount or a blast to your whole list. It takes the right message, sent at the right time, with one easy reason to come back. Here's how to do that, even if you don't have everyone's email or phone number.

Key takeaways

  • Decide who counts as inactive based on each customer's normal visit rhythm, not one cutoff for everyone.
  • Keep your "we miss you" message short, personal and specific about the next step.
  • Offer one single-use coupon that expires in about a week, so it creates urgency without training people to wait for deals.
  • In Canada, promotional texts and emails need consent under CASL. A wallet loyalty card adds lock-screen messages for customers who saved your card, and its sign-up page can include your own marketing consent checkbox.
  • Judge results by redemptions and by repeat visits after the coupon expires.

How long before a regular counts as inactive?

A customer is inactive when they've clearly broken their usual visit rhythm. Start from how often they normally come in, then adjust for the season and the type of customer.

Timing is what separates a welcome reminder from random marketing. Message too soon and you're bothering someone who was just busy. Wait too long and they've already found somewhere else.

BusinessTypical regularTreat as inactive after
CaféComes in most weekdaysAbout 2 weeks with no visit
Barber or salonBooks every 4 to 8 weeksAbout 10 weeks with no booking
RestaurantDines in every 4 to 6 weeksAbout 2 months with no visit

Treat these as starting points. Once you see who responds, you can tighten or loosen the window.

Spotting the customers who stopped coming

To find lapsed customers, compare each person's last visit to their normal rhythm. Then group them by what they usually come in for, so every message fits the person reading it.

Start with your last-visit data. A point-of-sale system usually holds the basics: customer name, last purchase date and what they bought. Pull everyone who has gone past your inactive window. Doing this once a month keeps the list manageable.

Check your wallet card data too. If you run a wallet loyalty card, you can export your cardholders for any date range with their reward and redemption activity included. Membership cards go further: they show each member's visit count and last visit, so you can sort by who hasn't been in.

Group by what they came for. Your morning coffee regular and your haircut regular don't want the same reminder. Small groups like "morning coffee," "colour appointments," "date-night dinners" or "family takeout" make your message feel written for them.

Note why people pause, when you know. If a customer has mentioned a move, a new job or a tight month, write it down. It helps you decide whether an offer is even the right move.

What should a "we miss you" message say?

A good win-back message tells the customer you noticed they've been away and gives them one thing to do right now. Noticing is what makes it feel personal rather than automated.

Include four things, in plain language:

  1. Acknowledge the gap. Say it's been a while.
  2. Reference what you know. Name the service or product they usually get.
  3. Give one reason to come back. A treat, a new item, an open spot this week.
  4. Say exactly what to do next. Redeem the coupon or book a time.

Keep it short enough to read in a few seconds on a phone. If someone has to think about what you mean, they'll scroll past.

A note for Canadian businesses: if you send promotional texts or emails, Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) generally requires the customer's consent. Keep a record of when and how each person agreed, and only send what they signed up for. This isn't legal advice, just a practical reminder.

Three sample messages

Café

"We miss you at [Café Name]! It's been a little while since your last coffee. Come by this week and your usual is on us."

Barber or salon

"Hi [Name], it's been a while since your last [service] at [Shop Name]. Here's a one-time comeback offer if you'd like to book in this week."

Restaurant

"We'd love to see you back at [Restaurant Name], [Name]. Show this at your table this week for a free [appetizer or dessert]."

Discount or something free?

The right comeback offer depends on your margins and on why the customer stopped coming. If price was the reason, a modest discount can help. If it was a schedule change or simple forgetfulness, a free add-on feels more generous and doesn't cheapen your regular prices.

Make it single-use, with an expiry. A one-time coupon that runs out in about a week gives people a real reason to come in soon. It also can't be saved and reused later, when they might have come back anyway.

If you discount, keep it contained. A flat amount off one category, or a small percentage off their usual order, protects your average sale better than a big blanket discount.

If you give something free, make it feel like you. A free drink with a meal, a quick style consultation or a sample with a purchase all fit naturally into what you already do.

If you're not sure which will work, test both: send half of one group the free add-on and the other half a small discount, then see which brings more people back.

Reaching lapsed customers without an email list

You don't need a perfect contact list to bring people back. A few channels work even when you're missing emails or phone numbers.

A wallet loyalty card. When a customer saves your loyalty card to Apple Wallet or Google Wallet, you can send a short message that shows up on their lock screen, with no app to download. You don't have to message everyone: tag the cardholders who've gone quiet and send the push to just that group. The comeback offer itself can be a single-use coupon card with an expiry date. Staff scan it once, and it can't be redeemed twice. This is especially useful for customers who paid cash or booked in person and never gave you an email.

The back of the card. Put this week's comeback offer or your booking link on the back of the card. When you change it, it updates on every card already saved, so there's nothing to reprint.

A "you're nearby" reminder. A wallet card can hold up to 10 of your locations, and the customer's phone may show a reminder on the lock screen when they're close by. On iPhone it shows your own message; Android phones show a general nearby notice. The location check happens on the phone, so nothing about where they go is sent to you.

Your storefront. A small sign near the till or in the window, such as "Haven't been in a while? Ask about your comeback offer," catches past customers who walk in without needing their inbox at all.

Ask one question when they come back. When a lapsed customer returns, ask: "What got in the way last time?" Sometimes the answer is parking, wait times or a menu change, and no coupon would have fixed that. Keep it to one quick question, in person or through a short form linked from a QR code.

Keeping your regulars on side

Loyal customers can feel overlooked if lapsed customers seem to get the better deal. A few habits keep a comeback offer from feeling unfair.

  • Give people time to miss you. One skipped visit isn't a lapse. Your inactive window keeps you from messaging people who are just busy.
  • Frame it as a thank-you. Position the coupon as a way to make returning easy, not as a reward for staying away.
  • Keep it one-time. A single-use offer with a short expiry avoids "deal dependency," where people hold off visiting until the next offer arrives.
  • Don't over-personalize. Mention their usual service or category if you know it. Skip anything that would make them wonder how you knew.
  • Treat your regulars too. Once the comeback campaign ends, consider a small thank-you offer for the customers who never left.

How to tell if it worked

A win-back campaign worked if lapsed customers come back, use the offer and keep visiting after it expires.

Count redemptions first. How many coupons were used tells you whether the message and timing landed. If the offer is a wallet coupon card, each redemption is recorded when staff scan it, so you can export the count instead of tallying it by hand.

Then watch the next few weeks. Look at repeat visits in the 2 to 6 weeks after the coupon expires. If people redeem once and disappear again, the offer got them in but something else needs fixing.

Compare groups, not totals. Look at café regulars separately from takeout customers, or colour clients separately from cuts. One combined number hides what's working.

What to countWhat it tells you
Coupons redeemedWhether the message and timing worked
Came back again after redeemingWhether the visit itself brought them back
Came back without using the couponWhether the reminder alone was enough
Didn't come backWhether the real reason was something an offer can't fix

Run one small campaign, learn from it, and adjust the next one.

Frequently asked questions

How soon should I reach out after someone stops coming?

Base it on their normal rhythm. For a weekday café regular, about 2 weeks. For a barber or salon client who books every 4 to 8 weeks, about 10 weeks. For a restaurant regular, about 2 months.

Can I win back customers without offering a discount?

Yes. A free add-on or a simple invitation to book often works better than a price cut, especially when the real reason they stopped was scheduling or forgetfulness.

Do I need text or email to run a win-back campaign in Canada?

No. If you do use texts or emails for promotions, CASL generally requires the customer's consent. A wallet loyalty card lets you send lock-screen messages to customers who saved your card, and you can add a marketing consent checkbox to its sign-up page.

What's the best way to create urgency?

Use a single-use coupon that expires in about a week. Customers know it won't last, and you avoid teaching them to wait for the next deal.

How long should I wait to judge the results?

Check redemptions as soon as the coupon expires. Then look at repeat visits over the following 2 to 6 weeks.
